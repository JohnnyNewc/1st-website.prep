The entire history of rome - The late empire

66 ce: Judaea rebels against Roman rule, beginning the First Jewish Revolt. Year of the Four Emperors and the Flavian dynasty (69–96 ce) 68–69 ce: Nero dies by suicide, and Rome is ravaged by civil war. Four leaders seize power in quick succession—Galba, Otho, Aulus Vitellius, and Vespasian. Each is defeated by his successor, and Vespasian, the final victor, establishes the Flavian dynasty and rules until 79 ce.
70 ce: Vespasian’s son Titus takes Jerusalem and burns the Temple, ending the First Jewish Revolt.
79 ce: Titus becomes emperor after his father’s death. Vesuvius erupts in the same year. Roman general Gnaeus Julius Agricola invades Scotland, having consolidated Roman rule of England and Wales as the province of Britannia.
80 ce: The Colosseum is inaugurated in grand fashion with 100 days of games.
81 ce: Titus dies suddenly at the age of 41 after reigning for just two years. His death is possibly engineered by his brother, Domitian, who succeeds him.
93–96 ce: Domitian unleashes a reign of terror against the Senate, executing many and expelling others regarded as “philosophers” for their Stoic views. A conspiracy against him, in which his wife, Domitia Longina, is complicit, is hatched after the execution of his cousin Flavius Clemens in 95 ce. Domitian is murdered by conspirators the following year. Five Good Emperors (96–180 ce) Hadrian HadrianWhite marble statue of the Roman emperor Hadrian, from an excavation at Sagalassos in southwest Turkey.
96 ce: Nerva is elected emperor by the Senate after Domitian’s murder. He adopts Trajan as coruler and successor the following year.
98 ce: Trajan becomes sole emperor after Nerva’s death. During his reign he extends the boundaries of the empire in the east.
101–114 ce: Trajan conquers Dacia and Armenia, advances into Arabia and Mesopotamia, and attacks Parthia.
117 ce: Trajan dies in Selinus and is succeeded by Hadrian, who consolidates and unifies the empire.
121–134 ce: Hadrian undertakes two tours of parts of the empire, including Gaul, Germany, Britain (where construction begins on Hadrian’s Wall), Spain, North Africa, Athens, and Egypt.
138–161 ce: Antoninus Pius succeeds Hadrian after his death. He suppresses rebellions in Britain, Mauretania, Germany, Dacia, and Egypt. He extends the British frontier into Scotland once again, with the construction of the Antonine Wall. The wall is abandoned in the 160s ce, and the army returns to Hadrian’s Wall.
161 ce: Antoninus Pius dies and is succeeded by Marcus Aurelius, who makes his adopted brother, Lucius Verus, co-emperor. Marcus is regarded by many as symbolic of the golden age of the empire.
167 or 168 ce: The emperors set off on an expedition across the Danube, during which Italy is besieged by German tribes. The attackers are repelled first by Marcus and Verus and then, after Verus’s death in 169 ce, by Marcus alone.
177 ce: Marcus appoints his 16-year-old son, Commodus, co-emperor, and they resume the wars against the Germanic tribes along the Danube.

Commodus, Year of Five Emperors, and the Severan dynasty (180–235 ce)
180 ce: Commodus becomes sole emperor after the death of Marcus Aurelius, comes to terms with the Germans, and returns to Rome. A plot against Commodus, led by his sister Lucilla, is discovered in 182 ce, and his rule thereafter becomes arbitrary and cruel.
192 ce: Commodus’s advisers have him strangled on December 31. By this time he has lapsed into insanity, imagining himself as Hercules and renaming Rome as Colonia Commodiana (Colony of Commodus). The Senate proclaims Publius Helvius Pertinax emperor.
193–194 ce: Pertinax is murdered less than three months into his reign, and over the next year four claimants fight for succession—Decimus Clodius Septimus Albinus, Pescennius Niger, Marcus Didius Severus Julianus, and Septimius Severus. The crisis ends with the eventual accession of Severus, although it is not until 197 ce that the last of the contenders, Albinus, is defeated.
211 ce: Caracalla, who had ruled jointly with his father, Severus, since 198 ce, becomes sole emperor after Severus dies. He proves to be a bloodthirsty tyrant, putting his wife and brother, among others, to death.
212 ce: Caracalla grants Roman citizenship to all free inhabitants of the empire.
217 ce: Severan rule is disrupted when Caracalla is murdered while fighting the Parthians. Macrinus, who led the plot against Caracalla, briefly seizes power.
218 ce: Macrinus becomes unpopular after securing a peace with the Parthians that is unfavorable to Rome and cutting soldiers’ pay. Elagabalus accedes to the throne, reestablishes the Severan dynasty, and captures and executes Macrinus, who had fled. Julia Maesa, Elagabalus’s grandmother and Caracalla’s aunt, is the real authority and persuades Elagabalus to adopt his cousin, Severus Alexander, as his son and heir. 222 ce: Elagabalus attempts to depose Alexander and is killed by his Praetorian Guard. Alexander succeeds him as emperor. Julia Maesa continues to hold the real power until her death in 226 ce. 230–235 ce: Persian King Ardashīr I invades the Roman province of Mesopotamia, and Alexander’s counteroffensive fails. However, heavy losses force the Persians to withdraw, allowing Alexander to claim victory. He then attempts to buy peace with the Alemanni, an invading Germanic tribe, alienating his army. Crisis of the Third Century (235–284 ce) 235 ce: Severus Alexander is assassinated by his soldiers, and a period of political instability follows the end of the Severan dynasty. Parts of the Roman Empire temporarily break off to form the Gallic Empire and the Palmyrene Empire, and Rome is ruled by a succession of more than 20 emperors in 50 years:

Diocletian
DiocletianDetail of a bust of Diocletian in the Capitoline Museum, Rome.
284 ce: Political stability is restored with the accession of Diocletian, who institutes an authoritarian system of government known as the dominate, in which the role of the emperor evolves from princeps, or “first citizen,” to dominus, or “master.” He reorganizes the fiscal, administrative, and military machinery of the empire, laying the foundation for the Byzantine Empire in the East and temporarily revitalizing the decaying empire in the West. The last persecution of Christians occurs during his reign.
293 ce: Diocletian establishes a tetrarchy, or rule of four, sharing power with Maximian (who had been made augustus in 286 ce) and appointing Constantius I and Galerius as caesars.
Fall of the Western Empire

305 ce: Diocletian and Maximian abdicate and are succeeded by Galerius and Constantius I. The empire is thrown into turmoil, with several claimants vying for the title of augustus, including Constantius’s son Constantine (who would rule as Constantine I, or Constantine the Great), Maximian’s son Maxentius, Licinius, and Galerius Valerius Maximinus.
312 ce: After some years of civil war Constantine I becomes Western emperor, and Licinius and Maximinus share power in the East. The following year Licinius defeats Maximinus and becomes sole emperor in the East. 313 ce: The Edict of Milan, establishing tolerance of Christianity throughout the empire, is proclaimed as the result of an agreement between Constantine I, the first Christian emperor, and Licinius.
324 ce: Constantine I attacks and defeats Licinius, unifying and becoming emperor of both the Western and Eastern empires. He Christianizes the empire, and he moves the capital to Constantinople (now Istanbul) in 330 ce. His bloodline continues but is unable to prevent the decline of the empire.
395 ce: The death of Theodosius I, the last ruler of a unified empire, leads to a renewed division between the Eastern and Western empires. The Western Empire, assailed by political turbulence, economic difficulties, and repeated invasion by Germanic tribes, had grown weak compared with the East, which had become wealthy from spices and other exports.
410 ce: The city of Rome is sacked by the Visigoths, severely destabilizing the Western Empire under Honorius, the younger son of Theodosius I.
451 ce: A combined force of Romans and Visigoths checks the advancement of Attila and the Huns in the Battle of the Catalaunian Plains.
476 ce: The fall of Rome is completed when German chieftain Odoacer defeats Romulus Augustulus, the last Western emperor.
